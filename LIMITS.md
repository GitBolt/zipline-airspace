# Limits

**What this is.** A measurement of one public, crowd-sourced feed over two operating areas for the same week: the published Dallas–Fort Worth area and the Austin area proposed in a draft assessment. It is not a collision-risk estimate and not a tracker of Zipline aircraft or sites. San Antonio and the other pending Texas metros are not included.

**The Austin boundary is traced, and it is a draft.** The draft assessment shows the Austin area only as a map, so the outline is traced from that map and is good to about 100 m. Its area matches the stated 2,498 sq mi, but a matching area does not prove every edge is in the right place. Traffic within a few hundred feet of the edge may be counted on the wrong side. It comes from a draft, and the final assessment may draw it differently.

**The feed is not an onboard receiver.** Ground feeders hear an aircraft under different geometry than a receiver at 330 ft a few thousand feet away. Where the feed's floor is high, a drone-mounted receiver may still hear a nearby aircraft perfectly well. How the two compare is the open question this survey cannot answer and Zipline's own logs can.

**Low traffic is undercounted where the feed cannot hear low.** The airport check shows reception to the ground near the centre of the metro and a floor of a few hundred feet at many outlying airports. Every traffic figure is what the feed heard, so the outer parts of the area are understated. That also biases the comparison between inside and outside the broadcast rule (the Mode C veil around DFW, Class C airspace around Austin-Bergstrom), because that is where reception is best.

**A cell without proof is not a cell without aircraft.** "Only heard higher" means no three aircraft were heard low there. It may be quiet, or the receivers may not reach.

**The no-broadcast share is a floor, not an estimate.** Aircraft with no transponder never appear. Multilateration needs several receivers in view at once, which is least likely exactly where reception is poor. TIS-B exists only where FAA radar sees the target. The true share is higher by an amount this data cannot bound.

**UAT is seen second-hand.** A 978 MHz aircraft shows up as ADS-R only while an FAA ground station is rebroadcasting it. UAT time is therefore also a lower bound.

**Heights carry about ±100 ft.** The on-runway check shows the size of the error. Results are tested against a 100 ft shift, but a 250 ft band is not much wider than that. Heights are above bare earth, so a helicopter over a rooftop pad reads high by about the building.

**The stand-off is drawn from public data.** Runway geometry comes from NASR. The exemption says "without suitable mitigations", so the stand-off is not a line Zipline never crosses, and the survey does not know what mitigations exist.

**One week is one week.** In Dallas–Fort Worth, light-aircraft activity swung by a factor of five between days, most likely with the weather. In Austin it swung by a factor of four and a half, but airline traffic also moved by 45%, so part of that swing may be the feed. Weekday and weekend rates rest on five and two days. The pipeline takes other dates.
